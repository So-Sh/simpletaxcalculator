<a id="what-you-pay"></a>
## What you are actually paying

Every gallon of gas carries several layers of tax:

- **Federal excise tax:** 18.4¢ per gallon for gasoline and 24.4¢ for diesel. It is the same in every state and has not changed since October 1, 1993.
- **State excise tax:** a cents-per-gallon charge set by each state. Most of this guide is about how this number is set and changed.
- **Other state charges:** environmental fees, inspection fees, and in some states a sales tax or wholesale tax on fuel.
- **Local taxes:** some counties and cities add their own.

You never pay gas tax at checkout. Distributors pay it before the fuel reaches the station, and the station builds it into the price on the sign.

<ExampleBlock label="Example">
Take a 15-gallon fill-up in Texas. State tax is 20¢ × 15 = $3.00. Federal tax is 18.4¢ × 15 = $2.76. That is $5.76 in taxes already inside the pump price.
</ExampleBlock>

New Jersey's excise rate looks like one of the lowest in the country. Once the other layers are counted, it is much closer to the middle of the pack. Even the totals can understate the cost. <a id="three-ways"></a>
## Three common ways states set or adjust their gas tax

States use many different designs, and these three are the most common. Many states combine them, so don't expect every state to fit neatly into one bucket.

### 1. Flat rates, changed only by lawmakers

A flat-rate state sets a cents-per-gallon number in law. It stays there until the legislature votes to change it.

- **Texas:** 20¢ per gallon, unchanged since October 1, 1991.
- **Alaska:** an 8¢ base rate unchanged since 1970, or 8.95¢ with fees.
- **Delaware:** 23¢, unchanged since 1995.
- **Arizona:** 18¢ for gasoline, unchanged since 1991.

The benefit is predictability. The drawback is that the tax loses value over time, which we cover below.

### 2. Indexed rates, adjusted by a formula

An indexed state ties its rate to a measure such as inflation, so the rate changes on a schedule without a new vote.

- **Florida:** adjusts every year based on the Consumer Price Index (CPI).
- **Virginia:** the base rate is indexed annually to CPI-U.
- **Michigan:** since January 1, 2026, it uses one inflation-indexed excise tax of 52.4¢.
- **Washington:** gasoline rises 2% a year for inflation, starting July 1, 2026.
- **North Carolina:** adjusts based on population change and a CPI energy index.
- **Minnesota:** adjusts annually based on a highway construction cost index.
- **California:** adjusts annually for inflation, each July 1.
- **Illinois:** adjusts annually for inflation, each July 1. The July 2026 increase was suspended through December 31, 2026.
- **Georgia:** adjusts each January 1 for inflation and fuel efficiency.

This is why a state can show up on a "gas tax changed this year" list even though no new law passed. Our [2026 gas tax changes report](/blog/gas-tax-changes-by-state-2026) shows how many states moved in a single year.

### 3. Market-linked rates, tied to fuel prices or wholesale values

In some states, the gas tax is linked partly to the price of fuel or another market measure. When that measure changes, the rate can change too. These changes usually come on a set schedule, not every time the pump price moves.

Utah is a good example. Its rate is adjusted annually using a rolling three-year average of wholesale fuel prices. A short price spike won't move the rate much, but a sustained rise or fall over several years will.

Other examples:

- **Nebraska:** a fixed tax plus wholesale-linked components, adjusted twice a year.
- **Indiana:** a gasoline use tax with a variable 7% share, reset monthly.
- **West Virginia:** a flat component plus a 5% wholesale price component, adjusted annually.

<a id="rate-vs-revenue"></a>
## Rate vs. revenue: why a tax increase isn't always a tax increase

Indexed taxes are often designed to keep road funding steady, not to make gas more expensive.

<HeroStat label="Texas gas tax rate" value="20¢" subtext="Unchanged since October 1, 1991" />

Consider the difference between nominal and real value. Texas's 20¢ rate has not changed since 1991, but consumer prices have more than doubled since then. The same 20¢ now buys far less road work than it did. Construction costs rise, cars get more fuel-efficient, and the tax collects less per mile driven.

<Callout type="info" title="Nominal vs. real tax rate">
  <p>A tax of 20¢ per gallon today doesn't buy the same amount of road funding that 20¢ did decades ago.</p>
</Callout>

That is one reason states move away from flat rates and toward indexing. If a state's rate is indexed to inflation, a small yearly increase may only maintain the tax's purchasing power. Washington's 2% annual adjustment is about 1.1¢ on a 55.4¢ rate. It is meant to keep pace with rising costs, not to add a new burden.

Headline changes can also mislead the other way. Michigan's excise rate jumped to 52.4¢ in 2026, but the state eliminated its 6% sales tax on fuel at the same time. The excise number alone overstates how much more drivers pay.

Why do states differ so much? Some lean more heavily on fuel taxes for roads than others. Political choices matter too. So does the shift toward more fuel-efficient and electric vehicles, which means each gallon-based tax dollar covers fewer miles of driving.

## How to find your next change date

1. Start with our [gas tax calculator](/gas-tax) for a national view, then open your state's page.
2. Check whether your state is flat, indexed, or market-linked, using the table above and your state's page.
3. For indexed and formula states, look for the adjustment date. Many adjust on January 1 or July 1. Iowa's most recent change, for example, took effect July 1, 2026. See the [Iowa gas tax page](/iowa/gas-tax) for details.
4. Check whether a temporary suspension applies to your state right now.
5. Check your state revenue department's site before making any decision that depends on an exact figure.

## The bottom line

A state's gas tax is set by one of a few common designs, and many states use more than one. Flat rates stay put until lawmakers act. Indexed and market-linked rates move on a schedule. The excise rate is only part of what you pay, so compare totals, not headlines. And in 2026, some states have paused the tax altogether for a few weeks, so check before you assume.
